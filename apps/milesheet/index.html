You probably don't deduct by the kilometre.

The per-kilometre rate you keep reading about is an employee reimbursement. If you drive for a living, you're self-employed — and you claim actual vehicle costs multiplied by how much of your driving was for work. That needs two things most apps only give you one of.

The number that isn't yours

Canada's rate for 2026 is 73¢ per kilometre for the first 5,000 km, then 67¢. It is widely quoted, widely apped, and it is the figure an employer uses to reimburse an employee. Sole proprietors — which is what you are if you drive for a platform — cannot multiply their business kilometres by it.

What you actually claim: fuel, insurance, maintenance, registration, lease or loan interest and depreciation — added up, then multiplied by your business-use percentage. Drive 12,380 km in a year with 8,420 of them for work and that percentage is 68%.

In the United States the standard mileage rate is real and you may use it — but it is a choice, and for drivers with high fuel, insurance and repair costs the actual-expense method is often worth more. Most apps only ever show you the standard rate.

Two halves, and you need both

Half one

A logbook proves the percentage

Date, destination, purpose and distance — for business and personal driving, because the percentage is a ratio. A missed trip doesn't lose one trip; it shifts the number every expense gets multiplied by.

Half two

Receipts prove the dollars

The percentage is worth nothing without the costs it applies to. Mileage apps track kilometres well and receipts badly. We came at this from the receipt side first.
